Communication in Pediatric Context

Definition: Communication is the exchange of meanings among people via a shared system of symbols (words, gestures, expressions).

It is both talking and listening (i.e., sending and receiving).Effective communication requires not just sending, but verifying that the receiver understood the meaning intended.

5Types of Communication: Verbal & Nonverbal

1. Verbal Communication

Verbal communication uses words (spoken or sometimes written). The effectiveness depends on clarity, relevance, timing, tone, pacing, and avoiding jargon.

Types of Communication: Verbal & Nonverbal

6Types of Communication: Verbal & Nonverbal

Characteristics of effective verbal communication:

Brief, clear, well-timed

Appropriate pacing — not too fast, not so slow that attention is lost

Avoid medical jargon, especially when speaking with children or caregivers

Repeat key points to reinforce essential messages

Check understanding (ask for feedback)

Warmth, interest, empathy conveyed in voice and phrasing

Avoid distractions, yes/no overuse, personal bias

Don’t monopolize conversation

Types of Communication: Verbal & Nonverbal

7Nonverbal Communication in Pediatrics

Nonverbal communication includes everything other than the words used: gestures, facial expressions, posture, eye contact, touch, spatial distance, timing, and tone. Many theorists believe that a large portion of meaning is carried nonverbally.

Nonverbal Communication in Pediatrics

8Nonverbal Communication in Pediatrics

Reasons nonverbal is powerful:

When verbal and nonverbal conflict, listeners tend to believe nonverbal cues.

Nonverbal cues often convey emotions, attitudes, and relational messages more than the words themselves.

Nonverbal is continuous, often subconscious, and cross-cultural (though interpretations vary)

Nonverbal Communication in Pediatrics

10Scenario:

A nurse enters a child’s room to explain a procedure. The nurse sits at the child’s eye level (nonverbal: reduces intimidation), uses a soft tone, and smiles gently. The nurse says, “I’m going to check your breathing for a few seconds.” The child nods. The mother asks, “Will it hurt?” The nurse pauses, touches the child’s shoulder (if acceptable), and answers truthfully, “You may feel a little pinch, but it will be fast, and I’ll be right here with you.” Because nonverbal and verbal align (gentle posture, reassurance), the child and parent are calmer.

3Pediatric Health History

Pediatric Health History

A health history is a collection of subjective data that provides a detailed

profile of the patient’s health status.

Nurses use therapeutic communication skills and interviewing techniques during the health history to establish an effective nurse–patient relationship and to gather data to identify actual and potential health problems.

(continued)

4Types of Pediatric Health Histories

Initial (comprehensive) health history:

An initial health care visit requires a comprehensive health history, which creates a database of information for the child who is new to the provider’s practice. The data collected in this history focus not just on past or current illnesses, but also on wellness, health patterns, health knowledge, and activities of daily living (ADL).

Types of Pediatric Health Histories

5Types of Pediatric Health Histories

Focused (episodic) health history:

This history is problem-centered, focusing on the present illness or problem. A focused health history is all that is necessary when the child presents with an acute problem or the exacerbation of a chronic condition.

Types of Pediatric Health Histories

6Types of Pediatric Health Histories

Follow-up health history:

When the child returns to the office after an illness or injury, a follow-up

history is taken to assess whether the problem is the same, better, or worse.

Types of Pediatric Health Histories

7Types of Pediatric Health Histories

Emergency health history:

An emergency health history involves the quick collection of data in

an acute situation. While this history resembles a focused history, it

needs to be obtained rapidly, usually simultaneously with

interventions, such as the administration of medications or respiratory treatments.

Types of Pediatric Health Histories

Speaker notes Plot on Percentile Charts: You plot these measurements on percentile charts, which are graphs that display a range of values from the lowest (0th percentile) to the highest (100th percentile). Each percentile line represents a specific percentage of the population. For example, the 50th percentile represents the median or middle value for the general population. Comparison: By plotting a child's measurements on these charts, you can see where their measurements fall in relation to the general population. For instance, if a child's height measurement is on the 75th percentile line, it means that their height is greater than or equal to 75% of children in the reference population. Interpretation: The interpretation of these percentile measurements depends on the specific parameter being assessed. For example, if a child consistently falls below the 5th percentile for weight, it might indicate underweight or slow growth, while measurements consistently above the 95th percentile might indicate overweight or rapid growth. However, it's essential to consider various factors, including genetics and individual growth patterns, when interpreting percentile data.

Ensure tissue oxygenation in conditions such as:

Abnormal pulmonary blood flow: (Persistent fetal circulation, Acute pulmonary hypertension)

Acute brain insults (e.g., increased intracranial pressure).

5Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition

Fluid Movement and Equilibrium:

Tonicity, hydrostatic pressure, and colloid osmotic pressure between plasma and interstitial fluid help maintain fluid balance.

At the arterial end of capillaries, water exits under hydrostatic pressure.

At the venous end, water is drawn back in by plasma oncotic pressure.

Speaker notes Tonicity: Tonicity refers to the relative concentration of solutes in a solution, influencing the direction of water movement across a semi-permeable membrane. Solutions can be isotonic (equal concentration), hypotonic (lower solute concentration outside the cell, causing water to move into the cell), or hypertonic (higher solute concentration outside, causing water to move out). Hydrostatic Pressure: This is the pressure exerted by a fluid in a closed system, such as blood inside blood vessels. In capillaries, hydrostatic pressure pushes water and small molecules out of the blood into the interstitial space, particularly at the arterial end of capillaries. Example: At the arterial end of capillaries, the blood's hydrostatic pressure is higher than the surrounding interstitial fluid, causing water and nutrients to filter out into surrounding tissues. Colloid Osmotic (Oncotic) Pressure: This pressure is generated by large proteins (primarily albumin) in the blood plasma, which exert a pulling force, drawing water back into capillaries. At the venous end of capillaries, colloid osmotic pressure becomes the dominant force, helping to draw water and waste products back into the bloodstream.

6Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition

Plasma Osmolality:

Plasma osmolality remains fairly constant, typically between 285 and 300 mOsmol/kg H₂O, helping maintain cellular homeostasis and fluid balance.

🔴 High Plasma Osmolality (> 300 mOsm/kg H₂O) – Dehydration

Causes: Excessive sweating, vomiting, diarrhea, uncontrolled diabetes.

Effects: Cells shrink due to water loss, leading to confusion, weakness, and possible coma.

🔵 Low Plasma Osmolality (< 285 mOsm/kg H₂O) – Overhydration

Causes: Excessive water intake, kidney disease, SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion).

Effects: Cells swell, which can lead to brain swelling (cerebral edema), headaches, seizures, and coma.

Speaker notes Plasma Osmolality and Its Role in Homeostasis Plasma osmolality refers to the concentration of solutes (such as sodium, glucose, and urea) in the blood plasma. It is crucial for maintaining fluid balance, cellular function, and overall homeostasis. 1. Normal Plasma Osmolality Range The normal range is 285–300 mOsmol/kg H₂O. This balance ensures that cells neither shrink (dehydrate) nor swell (overhydrate) due to fluid movement. 2. Factors That Affect Plasma Osmolality Plasma osmolality depends on: Sodium (Na⁺): The primary determinant, as sodium attracts water and regulates extracellular fluid balance. Glucose: Contributes to osmolality, especially in conditions like diabetes. Urea: A metabolic waste product that influences osmolality but does not drive water movement as strongly as sodium. 3. How Plasma Osmolality Regulates Fluid Balance To maintain osmolality within the normal range, the body uses two key mechanisms: ✅ 1. Thirst Mechanism (Water Intake) When osmolality increases (dehydration), the brain’s hypothalamus detects the change and triggers thirst. Drinking water dilutes plasma, restoring normal osmolality. ✅ 2. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) – Water Retention The pituitary gland releases ADH when osmolality is high. ADH signals the kidneys to retain water, reducing urine output and diluting plasma. When osmolality is low (overhydration), ADH secretion decreases, leading to more water excretion.
