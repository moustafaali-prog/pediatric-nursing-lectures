Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition is essential for understanding how fluids are distributed and regulated in the body. Here’s a breakdown:

Main Fluid Compartments:

Intracellular Fluid (ICF): Comprises about 40% of body weight.

Extracellular Fluid (ECF): Divided into:

Intravascular Fluid (plasma): Accounts for about 5% of body weight.

Interstitial Fluid: Makes up around 25% of body weight.

In a term newborn, water constitutes about 78% of body weight.

This decreases to approximately 60% in adults.

4Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition

Cell Membrane Permeability:

Cell membranes are relatively permeable to water, potassium (K⁺), and chloride (Cl⁻).

They are relatively impermeable to sodium (Na⁺), phosphate (PO₄³⁻), and proteins.

Sodium (Na⁺) is actively transported out of cells via sodium pumps, which require energy.

5Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition

Fluid Movement and Equilibrium:

Tonicity, hydrostatic pressure, and colloid osmotic pressure between plasma and interstitial fluid help maintain fluid balance.

At the arterial end of capillaries, water exits under hydrostatic pressure.

At the venous end, water is drawn back in by plasma oncotic pressure.

Speaker notes Tonicity: Tonicity refers to the relative concentration of solutes in a solution, influencing the direction of water movement across a semi-permeable membrane. Solutions can be isotonic (equal concentration), hypotonic (lower solute concentration outside the cell, causing water to move into the cell), or hypertonic (higher solute concentration outside, causing water to move out). Hydrostatic Pressure: This is the pressure exerted by a fluid in a closed system, such as blood inside blood vessels. In capillaries, hydrostatic pressure pushes water and small molecules out of the blood into the interstitial space, particularly at the arterial end of capillaries. Example: At the arterial end of capillaries, the blood's hydrostatic pressure is higher than the surrounding interstitial fluid, causing water and nutrients to filter out into surrounding tissues. Colloid Osmotic (Oncotic) Pressure: This pressure is generated by large proteins (primarily albumin) in the blood plasma, which exert a pulling force, drawing water back into capillaries. At the venous end of capillaries, colloid osmotic pressure becomes the dominant force, helping to draw water and waste products back into the bloodstream.

6Body fluid composition

Body fluid composition

Plasma Osmolality:

Plasma osmolality remains fairly constant, typically between 285 and 300 mOsmol/kg H₂O, helping maintain cellular homeostasis and fluid balance.

🔴 High Plasma Osmolality (> 300 mOsm/kg H₂O) – Dehydration

Causes: Excessive sweating, vomiting, diarrhea, uncontrolled diabetes.

Effects: Cells shrink due to water loss, leading to confusion, weakness, and possible coma.

🔵 Low Plasma Osmolality (< 285 mOsm/kg H₂O) – Overhydration

Causes: Excessive water intake, kidney disease, SIADH (syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion).

Effects: Cells swell, which can lead to brain swelling (cerebral edema), headaches, seizures, and coma.

Speaker notes Plasma Osmolality and Its Role in Homeostasis Plasma osmolality refers to the concentration of solutes (such as sodium, glucose, and urea) in the blood plasma. It is crucial for maintaining fluid balance, cellular function, and overall homeostasis. 1. Normal Plasma Osmolality Range The normal range is 285–300 mOsmol/kg H₂O. This balance ensures that cells neither shrink (dehydrate) nor swell (overhydrate) due to fluid movement. 2. Factors That Affect Plasma Osmolality Plasma osmolality depends on: Sodium (Na⁺): The primary determinant, as sodium attracts water and regulates extracellular fluid balance. Glucose: Contributes to osmolality, especially in conditions like diabetes. Urea: A metabolic waste product that influences osmolality but does not drive water movement as strongly as sodium. 3. How Plasma Osmolality Regulates Fluid Balance To maintain osmolality within the normal range, the body uses two key mechanisms: ✅ 1. Thirst Mechanism (Water Intake) When osmolality increases (dehydration), the brain’s hypothalamus detects the change and triggers thirst. Drinking water dilutes plasma, restoring normal osmolality. ✅ 2. Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH) – Water Retention The pituitary gland releases ADH when osmolality is high. ADH signals the kidneys to retain water, reducing urine output and diluting plasma. When osmolality is low (overhydration), ADH secretion decreases, leading to more water excretion.

16Fluid Deficit Calculation

Fluid Deficit Calculation

% Dehydration

This is an estimated percentage based on the degree of dehydration (mild, moderate, or severe).

It is calculated using weight loss or clinical signs.

weight loss Example:

If a child’s normal weight is 12 kg, but now weighs 11.2 kg, the weight loss is 0.8 kg.

% Dehydration = (0.8 kg ÷ 12 kg) × 100 = 6.7% (~7%).

17clinical signs

clinical signs

· Mild Dehydration (3-5% in infants, 3-4% in children):

Minimal symptoms; normal pulse, breathing, blood pressure, and behavior

Slight thirst; moist mucous membranes

Tears and urine output present

18clinical signs

clinical signs

· Moderate Dehydration (6-9% in infants, 6-8% in children):

Increased pulse, faster breathing (tachypnea)

Irritable, more thirsty

Dry mucous membranes, decreased tears

Delayed capillary refill (2-4 sec), oliguria (low urine output)

19clinical signs

clinical signs

· Severe Dehydration (≥10% in infants, 10% in children):

Very increased pulse, deep rapid breathing (hyperpnea), and possible shock

Hyperirritable or lethargic

Absent tears, sunken eyes, sunken fontanel

Very delayed capillary refill (>4 sec), cold/mottled skin

Oliguria or anuria (no urine production)

Fluid Deficit Calculation

Calculated fluid deficit (mL) = %dehydration ×weight (kg) × 10

e.g. 7% dehydration in a 12 kg child

= 7 × 12 × 10 = 840 mL deficit.

21How to Use This in Clinical Practice

How to Use This in Clinical Practice

· Assess the child’s dehydration status (mild, moderate, or severe).

· Determine weight and dehydration percentage based on clinical signs.

· Apply the formula to estimate fluid deficit.

· Plan fluid replacement therapy based on severity:

Mild: Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

Moderate/Severe: IV fluids (e.g., normal saline or Ringer's lactate)

221. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) for Mild to Moderate Dehydration

1. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) for Mild to Moderate Dehydration

Guidelines:

Mild dehydration → 50 mL/kg ORS over 4–6 hours.

Moderate dehydration → 100 mL/kg ORS over 4–6 hours.

Ongoing losses (e.g., diarrhea) → 10 mL/kg ORS per stool.

231. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) for Mild to Moderate Dehydration

1. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) for Mild to Moderate Dehydration

Key Considerations:

Administer small amounts (2–5 mL every 2–3 minutes) if the child refuses or has nausea.

Ondansetron (Zofran) may reduce vomiting and improve ORS tolerance.

Use commercial ORS solutions (e.g., Pedialyte, WHO ORS) to enhance sodium and water absorption.

For children resistant to drinking fluids, frozen ORS (popsicles) or flavoring agents can improve compliance.

Speaker notes 20 kcal/oz means that each ounce (oz) of breast milk or infant formula provides 20 kilocalories (kcal) of energy. Explanation: Kilocalories (kcal) measure energy from food. 1 ounce (oz) ≈ 30 mL Breast milk and standard infant formula contain 20 kcal per ounce, ensuring proper growth and development. 37Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Infants require higher fluid intake per body weight compared to older children and adults due to their higher metabolic rate, greater insensible water losses, and immature kidneys.

1. Fluid Requirements Based on Weight

General guideline: 75–90 mL per pound (mL/lb) per day

Alternate conversion: 2.5–3 ounces per pound (oz/lb) per day

38Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

2. Example Calculation for a 10 kg (22 lb) Infant

Step 1: Convert Body Weight to Pounds

10 kg = 22 lb

Step 2: Determine Fluid Needs in Ounces

Minimum: 22 lb×2.5 oz/lb=55oz/day

Maximum: 22 lb×3 oz/lb=66 oz/day

Thus, a 10 kg (22 lb) infant needs between 55–66 oz (1,650–1,980 mL) of fluid daily.

39Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

3. Fluid Intake and Caloric Needs

Since breast milk and infant formula provide both fluids and calories, we can estimate the energy intake:

Average caloric content of breast milk or formula = 20 kcal per ounce

Daily energy requirement = fluid intake (oz) × 20 kcal/oz

For our example:

Minimum kcal: 55×20=1,100 kcal/day

Maximum kcal: 66×20=1,320 kcal/day

Therefore, a 10 kg (22 lb) infant consumes approximately 1,100–1,320 kcal/day through breast milk or formula.

Slide 37 — Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Infants require higher fluid intake per body weight compared to older children and adults due to their higher metabolic rate, greater insensible water losses, and immature kidneys.

1. Fluid Requirements Based on Weight

General guideline: 75–90 mL per pound (mL/lb) per day

Alternate conversion: 2.5–3 ounces per pound (oz/lb) per day

Slide 38 — Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

2. Example Calculation for a 10 kg (22 lb) Infant

Step 1: Convert Body Weight to Pounds

10 kg = 22 lb

Step 2: Determine Fluid Needs in Ounces

Minimum: 22 lb×2.5 oz/lb=55oz/day

Maximum: 22 lb×3 oz/lb=66 oz/day

Thus, a 10 kg (22 lb) infant needs between 55–66 oz (1,650–1,980 mL) of fluid daily.

Slide 39 — Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

Daily Fluid Intake for Infants: Explanation and Calculation

3. Fluid Intake and Caloric Needs

Since breast milk and infant formula provide both fluids and calories, we can estimate the energy intake:

Average caloric content of breast milk or formula = 20 kcal per ounce

Daily energy requirement = fluid intake (oz) × 20 kcal/oz

For our example:

Minimum kcal: 55×20=1,100 kcal/day

Maximum kcal: 66×20=1,320 kcal/day

Therefore, a 10 kg (22 lb) infant consumes approximately 1,100–1,320 kcal/day through breast milk or formula.
